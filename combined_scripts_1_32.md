This man who fled from Pakistan went on to create one of Indian youngsters’ favorite motorcycles.
Har Prasad Nanda came to Delhi the Partition and started manufacturing tractors for farming. Later, he noticed that when it came to motorcycles, Indians had only two options, Royal Enfield Bullet and Java 250. Both were heavy and designed for European roads.
So in 1962, he took a license for the SHL motorcycle from a European company and redesigned it for Indian roads, launching it under the name Rajdoot, expecting it to be an instant hit. However, despite being cheaper and more durable, Rajdoot failed to beat Bullet and Java.
Then came an unexpected turning point. Raj Kapoor’s daughter married into the Nanda family, and Raj Kapoor featured the Rajdoot bike in his hit film Bobby, instantly making it popular among youngsters.
After this, Rajdoot 350 was launched in 1983, which was even used by the police. After ruling for nearly two decades, the arrival of Japanese brands like Honda led to the end of Rajdoot’s era by 2005. Still, Rajdoot is remembered today as one of India’s most iconic vintage motorcycles.

This is how the son of a vanaspati ghee maker became the king of the IT industry.

Azim was studying in college when his father suddenly passed away. At just 21 years of age, he had to return to India and take charge of his family’s vanaspati ghee business. Everyone believed he would fail.

But in 1977, Azim renamed the company Wipro and expanded beyond ghee into soaps, shoes, and electronics.
Then in 1978, the game completely changed when the Indian government forced IBM to exit India, even though it controlled nearly 80% of the IT market.

Azim spotted the opportunity and moved Wipro into IT services. He hired top engineers and focused on building long-term client relationships.
That decision transformed Wipro into one of the world’s leading IT giants, operating in 65+ countries and generating over $10 billion in revenue.

This man who fled from Pakistan went on to build one of India’s favorite biscuit brands.
Khanchand Ramnani ran a bakery in Pakistan, but after the partition, he was forced to leave everything behind and move to Hyderabad with his family. In 1953, he had to start again from absolute zero. In memory of the city he grew up in, he named his bakery Karachi Bakery.
With no money in the beginning, he first survived by buying goods from wholesalers and selling them. In the 1960s, he returned to his true passion and started baking again. His fruit biscuits and Osmania biscuits became so popular that people lined up outside the bakery every day.
That’s when the brand took off.
Today, Karachi Bakery produces 10 tons of biscuits daily and exports its handmade products to over 20 countries.

After a family partition, Haldiram’s grandson faced such a massive setback that he went on to build Haldiram’s biggest competitor. By selling bhujia at just 12 years old, Haldiram ji had built India’s largest namkeen empire by the 1960s. But after his death, the business was divided among four grandsons.
In this division, Shiv Ratan Agarwal received the smallest territory — the Bikaner region — which was only one-third the size of the Delhi division. Feeling betrayed and realizing that growth was impossible under the old restrictions, he decided to walk away from the family brand and start his own company, Bikaji.
That decision changed everything. Shiv Ratan invested heavily in technology, set up an automated bhujia factory, introduced better packaging, and even brought Amitabh Bachchan on board as the brand ambassador. As a result, today Bikaji generates over ₹2,600 crore in annual revenue and stands among the world’s biggest namkeen brands.
